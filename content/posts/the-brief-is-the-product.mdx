Most projects that go badly did not go wrong in execution. <mark>They went wrong in the first conversation, and everyone found out four weeks later.</mark>

The work was competent. It answered a question nobody had checked was the right one.

## Requests are not problems

A client asks for a redesign. That is a request. The problem might be that traffic converts poorly, or that sales spends an hour a week explaining what the company does, or that a competitor launched something that made the founder anxious on a Sunday.

Those are four different projects. <mark>A redesign solves exactly one of them, and possibly none.</mark>

Taking the request at face value feels respectful and efficient. It is neither, because it defers the real conversation until after the work exists, when changing direction is expensive and everyone is invested.

## The questions that pay for themselves

**What happens if we do nothing?** If the answer is "not much," you have found the actual priority, and it is somewhere else.

**Who decides this is finished?** If that person is not in the kickoff, they will appear in week six with opinions that were always going to arrive.

**What does this need to do, in numbers?** "Feel more premium" cannot be evaluated, so it can be relitigated forever. "Reduce drop-off on the pricing page" can be checked.

**What has already been tried?** There is usually a previous attempt. The reason it failed is the most useful information available and almost nobody volunteers it.

<mark>An hour of this is worth more than a week of revisions</mark>, because revisions cannot fix a misdiagnosis. They just make it more polished.

## Agreement is not alignment

The most dangerous moment in a project is when everyone nods.

Words like "clean," "modern," "premium," and "bold" mean different things to every person in the room, and they all sound agreed upon. Two people can nod at "premium" while picturing opposite outcomes, and neither discovers it until something concrete exists to disagree about.

<mark>Vague agreement is a disagreement that has not happened yet.</mark> The fix is not more meetings, it is specificity early enough that disagreement is cheap. References, numbers, and explicit non-goals do more than another round of discussion.

## Writing it down is the work

The brief is not documentation of the thinking. It is the thinking, and it is not done until it is written.

Writing forces the vagueness into the open. You cannot write "modern but timeless, bold but understated" and not notice that it constrains nothing. <mark>A brief that could describe any project is not a brief, it is a mood.</mark>

The useful version is short and specific: what problem, for whom, judged how, and what is explicitly out of scope. If it does not include something you are choosing not to do, it is not finished.

## Where this actually costs you

The pressure always runs toward starting. Briefs feel like overhead, clients want to see progress, and nobody was ever thanked for spending a week on a document.

But <mark>the cost of a wrong brief is not the brief, it is everything built on top of it.</mark> That bill arrives late, and it is always larger than the time you saved.
