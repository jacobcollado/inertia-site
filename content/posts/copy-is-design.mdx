Design reviews talk about spacing, hierarchy, and color. They rarely talk about the words, even though <mark>the words are the first thing anyone actually processes.</mark>

Nobody looks at a page. They read it, decide what it is, and then decide whether to stay. That happens before any deliberate evaluation of the design.

## Lorem ipsum lies to you

A layout built on placeholder text is a layout built on a fiction: that all content is roughly the same length, evenly distributed, and free of awkward edge cases.

Real copy is nothing like that. One product name is three characters, another is thirty-one. One testimonial is a sentence, the next is a paragraph. <mark>A design that only works with the content you invented is not a design, it is an illustration of one.</mark>

Design with the real words, or the worst-case real words. The layout that survives "Allure New York" and "Ftg" in the same column is the one that ships.

## Tone is a visual property

Two buttons, identical in every measurable way. One says "Submit." The other says "Send it over."

Same pixels, different products. <mark>Voice changes what a component feels like more reliably than most styling decisions do.</mark> You can spend a week on a button's hover state and undo all of it with the wrong verb.

This is why handing copy off at the end is backwards. By then the layout has already committed to a tone, and the writer is asked to fill boxes that were shaped by placeholder text.

## Length is layout

A heading that runs to three lines on mobile is not a copy problem to be solved later. It is a layout that has not been designed yet.

The interesting constraint runs both ways. Sometimes the right answer is a wider column. Often the right answer is <mark>a shorter heading, because the long one was hiding an unclear idea.</mark> Copy that will not fit is frequently copy that has not been decided.

Cutting a heading from eleven words to four usually improves both the layout and the thinking behind it.

## The parts nobody writes

Button labels. Error messages. Empty states. Form validation. Placeholder text. Confirmation toasts.

These are almost never written, they accumulate. Someone types "Something went wrong" at 6pm and it lives in production for three years. <mark>Users encounter this writing at exactly the moments they are most likely to leave</mark>, which makes it the least appropriate place for a default.

An error that says what happened and what to do next is worth more than most visual polish. It is also cheaper.

## The practical version

Write the copy first, even badly. Design against it. Rewrite when the layout teaches you something. Rewrite again when someone reads it out loud and stumbles.

<mark>If reading it aloud is embarrassing, it is not finished</mark>, regardless of how it looks on screen.
