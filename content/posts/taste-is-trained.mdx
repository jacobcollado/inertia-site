Taste gets talked about like a trait people are born with. Either you have an eye or you do not.

I do not believe that, mostly because I did not have one. <mark>I started with no design background and no idea what I was looking at.</mark> What I had instead was volume, repetition, and enough time to notice patterns.

## What taste actually is

Taste is not preference. Preference is what you like. Taste is being able to tell what works, including when it is not what you like.

That distinction matters because it makes taste testable. <mark>A trained eye can look at two versions and say which one is better and why</mark>, and be right about it in a way that holds up when other people look too. That is a skill, not an opinion.

It usually presents as instant recognition. Someone glances at a layout and knows something is off before they can explain it. The speed makes it look innate. It is not. It is compression.

## Exposure has to be deliberate

Scrolling through good work does not train anything. You have seen thousands of well-designed things and it did not make you better, because looking is passive.

The version that works is slower and more annoying. <mark>You find something that works, and you do not move on until you can say why.</mark> Which decision is carrying it? What would break if you changed the type? Why does the spacing feel deliberate rather than arbitrary?

Most of the time you will be wrong about the reason. That is fine. Being wrong out loud is what makes the next observation sharper. Being vaguely impressed teaches you nothing.

## Bad work is the better teacher

Studying good design tells you what success looks like. It rarely tells you what caused it, because in finished work every decision looks inevitable.

Broken work is more legible. <mark>When something feels cheap, the cause is usually identifiable</mark>: inconsistent spacing, too many type sizes, a hierarchy that fights itself, four different greys where one would do. The failure is visible in a way that success is not.

You learn more from diagnosing one bad page than from admiring ten good ones.

## Reps are the mechanism

None of this substitutes for making things. You have to build the thing, ship it, and see it in the world where you have no control over context.

Something you thought was resolved looks amateur three months later. That gap is the actual training signal. <mark>It means your eye developed faster than your last project did</mark>, which is the only evidence of progress you get.

If you look back at work from a year ago and still think it is great, that is not a good sign.

## The honest timeline

This takes years, and it takes them regardless of how efficiently you approach it. You cannot compress the part where you have to be wrong repeatedly and find out about it.

<mark>The only comparison worth making is against yourself a year ago.</mark> Measuring against people ten years in is a category error, not a motivational problem.

The encouraging part is that it is learnable at all. Nobody has an eye at the start. Everyone who has one built it, usually while assuming everyone else was born with theirs.
