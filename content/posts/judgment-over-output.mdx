The constraint on creative work used to be production. Making the thing was slow, so being able to make it was the skill.

That constraint is mostly gone. <mark>What is scarce now is knowing which of the twenty options in front of you is the right one</mark>, and that has not gotten cheaper at all.

## Volume was never the bottleneck

I can generate forty homepage directions in an afternoon. This sounds like leverage. Mostly it is a harder problem than having four.

Forty options do not contain more good ideas than four. They contain the same few good ideas plus thirty-six ways to waste a week. <mark>The work moved from producing options to eliminating them</mark>, and elimination is the part that requires taste.

Anyone can add. Knowing what to remove is the skill that took years.

## Judgment is compressed experience

When someone looks at a layout and says "the spacing is wrong," they are not consulting a rule. They are pattern-matching against every layout they have ever seen, built, and shipped, including the ones that failed.

<mark>That is not intuition. It is a compressed history of consequences.</mark> They have watched a hero like this one underperform. They have seen this exact type pairing look great in a mockup and fall apart in production. The recall feels instant because the cost was paid years earlier.

There is no shortcut through that, because the input is not information. It is outcomes.

## What tools actually change

A good tool moves you from a blank page to a starting point in seconds. That is genuinely valuable, and it is not the same as moving you toward a finished thing.

<mark>The starting point is only useful if you can tell what is wrong with it.</mark> Without that, faster generation just produces a larger pile of plausible work, and plausible is the most dangerous quality a design can have. It survives review. It reads as fine. It does not work, and nobody can say why.

The people getting the most out of these tools are the ones who already knew what good looked like. The tools made them faster. They did not make them right.

## Reps are the whole method

There is no interesting answer to how you develop judgment. You do the work, you ship it, you watch what happens, and you carry the result into the next one.

The essential part is the watching. <mark>Work you never shipped teaches you almost nothing</mark>, because you never found out if you were right. Ten shipped projects are worth more than a hundred concepts, and one project that failed publicly is worth more than either.

That is also why it cannot be borrowed. You can read someone else's principles, but you cannot inherit the specific memory of the time you were confident and wrong.

## The part that stays hard

The uncomfortable version: the tools will keep getting better at the part I used to be paid for, and they will not get better at deciding what should exist.

<mark>Being able to make anything is only useful if you know what is worth making.</mark> That has always been the job. It was just easier to hide behind execution when execution was slow.
